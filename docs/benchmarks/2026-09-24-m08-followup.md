# M08 follow-up: sequential delivery slowdown and seek recovery

Follow-up to the [15-source comparison](2026-09-24-browser-origin-head.md), measured on 2026-09-24. M08 was selected because it had the worst candidate-only playback regression: a 51.19-second seek followed by five waiting events, versus a 10.98-second seek and no waits on baseline. The source is UHD HEVC/HDR with TrueHD, using hardware H.264 transcoding and tone mapping for the browser.

The subsequent [transport isolation](2026-09-24-m08-transport-isolation.md)
reproduces the delivery collapse with plain TCP, rejects a larger-credit fix,
and verifies session-lifetime cancellation for detached seeks.

## Findings

The traced replay does **not** show another alternating-offset/interleave pathology. After header/index probing, opening reads advance monotonically through one window; post-seek reads advance monotonically through the other. There is an intermittent delivery slowdown, plus a deadline mismatch that amplifies sufficiently slow seeks. The exact transport/backpressure trigger is still unproven.

The same candidate release (`59b4ebd`) and matching NAS mediahost were replayed through the browser, with source-demand tracing enabled and the same two 120-second observation windows. No application behavior or buffer sizes were changed.

| Measurement | Original candidate run | Traced candidate replay |
|---|---:|---:|
| Startup | 5.82 s | 4.31 s |
| Seek to 75% | 51.19 s | 8.63 s |
| Opening waiting events | 0 | 0 |
| Post-seek waiting events | 5 | 0 |
| Post-seek advancement in 120 wall seconds | 82.03 s | 119.96 s |

This does not reproduce the original seek failure deterministically. It does reproduce a substantial delivery-rate collapse during the opening window, without any generation churn at that point.

## What the read trace shows

Between 14:40:50 and 14:41:30 UTC, the active opening window advances roughly 177–183 MiB per ten seconds. After the transition, between 14:41:40 and 14:42:50, it advances only roughly 18 MiB per ten seconds: about **18 → 1.8 MiB/s**. The reads remain forward-only. The source-demand wait counter consumes almost all elapsed time in these intervals.

There are no backward offsets among 7,022 reads in the opening data region (10,026,778 through 986,302,878), or among 16,976 reads in the post-seek data region (50,168,284,708 through 52,590,126,489). Header/index probing and the deliberate user seek are excluded from these counts. After the user seek, the other window supplies roughly 18 MiB/s for the rest of the replay, again without backward jumps.

The client buffer covers the opening slowdown during this observation window but drains from roughly one minute to 22 seconds. Therefore, no waiting event in this replay does not mean the producer stayed healthy throughout.

TCP snapshots are consistent with connection-specific delivery degradation: the slow byte connection's measured RTT rose to approximately 119 ms; the active post-seek connection showed about 10 ms. The slow connection had queued outbound bytes at the NAS. These observations implicate the delivery path, but do not establish why its TCP timing changed, or distinguish HTTP/2 credit feedback from every other possible cause.

## Storage and byte-only controls

Read-only controls used the same opening and deep-seek file regions after replay:

- NAS positional reads of 128 MiB took 0.245 and 0.320 seconds: approximately 523 and 399 MiB/s. These were warm-cache measurements, not cold-disk benchmarks.
- A 256 MiB SSH transfer took 11.62 seconds of remote transfer time, approximately 22 MiB/s.
- Candidate direct sessions transferred 512 MiB from each region, without GStreamer or `ReadAhead`. Each transfer deliberately stopped consumption for five seconds after the first 128 MiB. The sixteen 64 MiB samples ranged from 17.26 to 23.22 MiB/s, excluding the deliberate pause. This control did **not** reproduce the tenfold slowdown.

Thus ordinary sequential reads and a simple consumer pause are insufficient to reproduce the bad state. The sustained playback consumer and persistent source-connection behavior need closer isolation. It would be premature to increase the retained windows or change eviction on this evidence.

## Why the original seek took 51 seconds

The original hub log provides this sequence (UTC):

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| 12:35:33.166 | Seek replacement worker starts |
| 12:35:58.002 | Browser ends the session, approximately 25 seconds later |
| 12:36:03.303 | Hub readiness attempt times out and starts fallback sink |
| 12:36:15.125 | Late run is dropped; its worker is killed |
| 12:36:15.184 | Browser replacement session starts |
| 12:36:23.977 | Replacement session becomes ready |

The player starts its [25-second give-up timer](../../web/src/components/Picture.vue) before awaiting the seek POST. Giving up marks the session dead, and a watcher sends `endSession`. The executor permits [30 seconds per readiness attempt](../../crates/kahawai-playback/src/executor.rs), and the [seek handler](../../crates/kahawai-hub/src/sessions/seek.rs) can then retry using a fallback sink. An in-flight attempt continues after the session has been removed. The recovered session's startup adds another approximately nine seconds.

The saved failed-run playlist contains only one 2.002-second segment, below the [6.5-second minimum runway](../../crates/kahawai-playback/src/playlist.rs). This explains why “some output exists” did not satisfy readiness. It does not explain the original delivery slowdown by itself.

A slow source can therefore trigger a client/server deadline race and redundant recovery work. The next changes should address cancellation and consistent recovery deadlines separately from investigating the byte-delivery collapse. Merely enlarging the client timeout would hide part of the symptom and leave slow production unresolved.

## Evidence and restoration

Private evidence lives in `target/benchmarks/2026-09-24-m08-diagnosis/`: `head-trace/` holds the browser replay and source trace; `reads.json` contains paired demand/completion records; `io-control.json` and `direct.json` hold the controls; `observations.json` records TCP snapshots and final restoration checks. The scripts are environment-specific and retained for auditing. Source identities remain in the private benchmark manifest.

Both diagnostic runs restored the original local AIO and NAS mediahost binaries. Final hashes match the backups, both local and Mac mini hubs report the NAS healthy, and no diagnostic sessions remain. The Mac mini executable was not changed. No production-code changes were made.
